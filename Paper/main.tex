\begin{abstract}
Bitcoin CoinJoin transactions deliberately obscure payment flows
by merging unrelated users' unspent transaction outputs into a
single on-chain record, significantly weakening the
Common~Input~Ownership~Heuristic that underpins most
blockchain forensic tools. Existing detection methods suffer
from three compounding limitations: structural clustering fails
under Taproot's Pay-to-Taproot script indistinguishability,
CoinJoin Spending Transaction linking relies on
one-dimensional temporal proximity and ignores structural
signals, and supervised classifiers produce poor minority-class
recall on severely imbalanced blockchain datasets.
This study introduces MixTrace, a multi-modal framework that
addresses all three limitations concurrently.
A Taproot-Aware DBSCAN strategy reduces the false-positive
rate of naive heuristic clustering by 79.8 percent across
200,000 analysed transactions.
A four-dimensional Chamfer Distance formulation extends
temporal CST linking to the joint space of timestamp,
fee rate, input count, and output count.
Deep Graph Infomax pre-training on all 203,769 Elliptic
nodes, followed by Graph Attention Network fine-tuning
with class-weighted cross-entropy, achieves an illicit-class
F1-score of 0.7337, precision of 0.7094, recall of 0.7598,
and ROC-AUC of 0.9868 on the held-out test set,
surpassing Random Forest (F1: 0.6285),
K-Nearest Neighbours (F1: 0.4829),
K-Means (F1: 0.0883),
and a standalone Graph Attention Network without
pre-training (F1: 0.2037).
The complete reproducible pipeline is publicly available to
support forensic and compliance research.

\keywords{Bitcoin \and CoinJoin \and Graph Attention Network
  \and Deep Graph Infomax \and Blockchain forensics
  \and Chamfer distance \and Illicit transaction detection
  \and ADASYN oversampling}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
%% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The global proliferation of cryptocurrency-facilitated crime,
including ransomware payments, darknet market settlements, and
sanctions evasion, has accelerated demand for robust blockchain
forensic tools~\citep{chainanalysis2023crime}.
Bitcoin, the oldest and most liquid cryptocurrency, operates
on a transparent public ledger, yet its pseudonymous address
model provides meaningful privacy when users take deliberate
countermeasures.
CoinJoin~\citep{maxwell2013coinjoin} is the most widely
deployed on-chain privacy mechanism: multiple independent
parties collaboratively construct a single transaction that
merges their unspent transaction outputs, breaking the
address-clustering assumption known as the
Common~Input~Ownership~Heuristic
(CIOH)~\citep{meiklejohn2013fistful}.
Wallets including Wasabi and JoinMarket have collectively
processed hundreds of millions of dollars through CoinJoin
coordination, making its deanonymization a priority for
financial intelligence units~\citep{möser2017empirical}.

Three research gaps persist in the literature that limit the
effectiveness of automated CoinJoin analysis.

\textbf{Gap 1 -- Taproot Clustering Failure.}
Bitcoin's Taproot soft fork, activated at block 709,632 in
November 2021, introduced Pay-to-Taproot (P2TR) scripts based
on Schnorr signatures and key aggregation via
MuSig2~\citep{btcbip341}.
A single-user P2TR output is computationally indistinguishable
on-chain from a multi-party aggregated P2TR output used in
CoinJoin coordination.
Consequently, naive CIOH clustering applied to Taproot
transactions produces systematic false positives: legitimate
single-user P2TR transactions are incorrectly flagged as
collaborative mixing events.
No prior study has proposed a structural clustering remedy
tailored to this post-Taproot forensic environment.

\textbf{Gap 2 -- One-Dimensional CST Linking.}
CoinJoin Spending Transaction (CST) deanonymization requires
linking the outputs of a mixing event to the subsequent
spending transactions of individual participants.
The only published similarity metric for this task uses a
one-dimensional Chamfer distance computed over
timestamps~\citep{trautman2021similarity}.
Fee-rate behaviour, input multiplicity, and output cardinality
carry independent structural signals that remain entirely
unexploited in current linking pipelines.

\textbf{Gap 3 -- Class Imbalance in Illicit Detection.}
The Elliptic Bitcoin Dataset~\citep{weber2019anti}, the
standard benchmark for illicit node detection, contains
4,545 illicit and 42,019 licit labelled transactions,
yielding a 9.2:1 class-imbalance ratio.
Standard graph classifiers learn to maximise overall accuracy
by suppressing minority-class predictions, producing precision
values below 0.50 on the illicit class for most published
baselines.
The 157,205 unlabelled nodes in the dataset represent a
large reservoir of structural information that supervised
methods discard by design.

To address these three gaps simultaneously, the present study
introduces \textbf{MixTrace}, a multi-modal framework
consisting of three coordinated components:
(i)~a Taproot-Aware DBSCAN clustering strategy that exploits
structural and behavioural transaction features to reduce
false-positive clustering errors;
(ii)~a Multi-Dimensional Chamfer Distance (MD-CD) formulation
that extends the CST linking metric from one dimension to
four dimensions; and
(iii)~a two-phase graph learning architecture that applies
Deep Graph Infomax (DGI) self-supervised pre-training across
all graph nodes before fine-tuning a Graph Attention Network
(GAT) with class-weighted cross-entropy and ADASYN
oversampling.

Experiments are conducted on two datasets: the public Elliptic
Bitcoin Dataset and an author-curated corpus of 5,884,387
Bitcoin transactions enriched with nine novel engineered
features.
Results demonstrate that MixTrace achieves an illicit-class
F1-score of 0.7337 and ROC-AUC of 0.9868, representing
improvements of 10.5 percentage points in F1 over the
best-performing baseline (Random Forest) and a 79.8 percent
reduction in false-positive rate compared to naive CIOH.

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows.
Section~\ref{sec:related} reviews the relevant literature.
Section~\ref{sec:method} presents the MixTrace methodology in
full detail.
Section~\ref{sec:exp} describes the experimental setup and
presents results.
Section~\ref{sec:discuss} analyses findings critically.
Section~\ref{sec:conclude} summarises contributions and
identifies directions for future research.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
%% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\subsection{Bitcoin Privacy and CoinJoin Detection}

The foundational analysis of Bitcoin address clustering was
conducted by~\citet{meiklejohn2013fistful}, who demonstrated
that applying the CIOH to co-spending inputs at scale could
identify thousands of entity clusters, including major
exchanges and darknet markets.
Subsequent empirical studies confirmed that commercial
blockchain analytics platforms derive a substantial fraction
of address attribution from CIOH-based
clustering~\citep{goldfeder2018cookie}.
\citet{bistarelli2019analysis} applied community-detection
algorithms to Bitcoin transaction graphs and achieved
clustering purities above 70 percent for known CoinJoin
participants, but the study predates Taproot activation and
does not model script-type heterogeneity.
\citet{möser2017empirical} quantified de-anonymisation risk
for Mixing Services over a multi-year period, finding that
structural heuristics can substantially reduce anonymity
even when users complete full mixing rounds.
None of these studies account for the structural ambiguity
that P2TR scripts introduce, leaving the post-Taproot
clustering problem open.

\subsection{Graph Neural Networks in Blockchain Forensics}

\citet{weber2019anti} introduced the Elliptic transaction
dataset and evaluated a graph convolutional network (GCN)
baseline alongside logistic regression and random forest
classifiers.
The GCN achieved an illicit-class F1 of approximately 0.70
on the test set, establishing the primary benchmark.
\citet{alarab2020competence} investigated deep neural networks
with balanced class weighting on the same dataset but did not
exploit the unlabelled node majority, limiting the information
available to the learned representation.
\citet{chen2020phishing} demonstrated that multi-head graph
attention on Ethereum transaction graphs yields significant
improvement over non-attention GCN variants for phishing
detection, motivating the use of GAT architectures in the
present study.
\citet{lo2023inspection} reviewed machine-learning methods
applied to anti-money laundering across multiple blockchain
platforms and identified self-supervised pre-training as an
under-explored direction for addressing label scarcity in
blockchain forensics datasets.

\subsection{Self-Supervised Graph Representation Learning}

Deep Graph Infomax (DGI)~\citep{velickovic2019deep} proposes
maximising the mutual information between local node
representations and a global graph summary by training a
bilinear discriminator to distinguish real node-summary pairs
from corrupted pairs obtained via row-shuffling of the feature
matrix.
Since the objective is unsupervised, DGI can leverage the
entire graph including unlabelled nodes, making it
particularly well-suited to the Elliptic dataset where
75.7 percent of nodes carry no label.
\citet{you2020graph} demonstrated that contrastive
self-supervised pre-training consistently improves downstream
classification performance on node-level tasks, particularly
when labelled data is sparse or heavily imbalanced.

\subsection{Transaction Linking via Chamfer Distance}

\citet{trautman2021similarity} introduced a one-sided Chamfer
distance measure applied to the timestamps of candidate
CoinJoin spending transactions.
The one-sided formulation assigns a distance from a source
window to a target window as the mean of each source point's
minimum distance to any target point, capturing asymmetric
temporal proximity between spending clusters.
The study evaluated the metric on a manually annotated
dataset of Wasabi CoinJoin outputs but restricted the
feature space to a single dimension.
The present study retains the one-sided Chamfer formulation
and extends the feature space to four dimensions, yielding
the MD-CD contribution described in Section~\ref{sec:mdcd}.

\subsection{Class Imbalance Mitigation}

Adaptive Synthetic Sampling (ADASYN)~\citep{he2008adasyn}
generates synthetic minority-class samples with density
proportional to the local imbalance in feature space,
focusing synthesis on regions where the classifier is likely
to misclassify.
ADASYN consistently outperforms random oversampling and
SMOTE on highly skewed distributions~\citep{chawla2002smote}.
In the present study, ADASYN is applied to the labelled node
feature matrix as a diagnostic reference, while the primary
class-imbalance mitigation strategy is inverse-frequency
class weighting applied directly to the graph cross-entropy
loss, which preserves the graph-structural relationships
that ADASYN cannot encode.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}
%% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\subsection{Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:problem}

Let $\mathcal{G} = (\mathcal{V}, \mathcal{E}, \mathbf{X})$
denote the Elliptic transaction graph, where
$\mathcal{V}$ is the set of $N = 203{,}769$ transaction nodes,
$\mathcal{E}$ is the set of 234,355 directed edges
representing Bitcoin payment flows, and
$\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times F}$ is the node feature
matrix with $F = 169$ dimensions (165 original Elliptic
features plus four novel graph-structural features introduced
in Section~\ref{sec:features}).
Each node $v \in \mathcal{V}$ is associated with a discrete
time step $\tau(v) \in \{1, \ldots, 49\}$ and a class label
$y_v \in \{1, 0, -1\}$, where 1 denotes illicit, 0 denotes
licit, and $-1$ denotes unknown.
The labelled subset contains 46,564 nodes; the remaining
157,205 nodes are unlabelled.

The learning objective is binary node classification on the
labelled subset, evaluated on nodes with $\tau(v) \in
\{42,\ldots,49\}$ (the test partition) using precision,
recall, F1-score, PR-AUC, and ROC-AUC on the illicit class.

\subsection{Novel Feature Engineering}
\label{sec:features}

Nine novel features are computed across the two datasets to
supplement existing representations.

\subsubsection{Author Dataset: Five Transaction-Level Features}

\paragraph{Denomination Entropy (DE).}
CoinJoin transactions tend to produce multiple outputs of
equal value to obscure the payment-to-change distinction.
For a transaction $t$ with output value vector
$\mathbf{v} = (v_1, \ldots, v_k)$, the normalised probability
vector is $p_i = v_i / \sum_j v_j$.
The denomination entropy is:
\begin{equation}
  \Entropy(t) = -\sum_{i=1}^{k} p_i \log_2 p_i.
  \label{eq:entropy}
\end{equation}
High entropy ($\Entropy \to \log_2 k$) indicates equal-valued
outputs, a strong CoinJoin structural signature.

\paragraph{Mixing Index (MI).}
The combinatorial ambiguity of input-output pairing is
captured by:
\begin{equation}
  \text{MI}(t) = \frac{|\mathcal{A}| \cdot |\mathcal{B}|}{|\mathcal{A} \cup \mathcal{B}|},
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{A}$ and $\mathcal{B}$ are the input and
output address sets.
In the author dataset, MI exhibits a mean of 8.79 and
standard deviation of 689.27, reflecting the presence of
high-fan-out mixing transactions.

\paragraph{Temporal Irregularity (TI).}
Mixing services often coordinate transactions at atypical
hours to reduce mempool competition.
TI is computed as the $z$-score of a transaction's fee rate
within the cell defined by its hour-of-day $h$ and
day-of-week $d$:
\begin{equation}
  \text{TI}(t) = \frac{f_t - \mu_{h,d}}{\sigma_{h,d}},
\end{equation}
where $f_t$ is the fee rate in sat/vbyte and $\mu_{h,d}$,
$\sigma_{h,d}$ are the cell mean and standard deviation.

\paragraph{UTXO Concentration (UC).}
A Herfindahl-Hirschman Index approximation measures output
value skewness:
\begin{equation}
  \text{UC}(t) = r^2,
\end{equation}
where $r$ is the value-concentration ratio.
CoinJoin outputs are expected to have low concentration,
yielding UC near zero; single-payment transactions exhibit
UC near one.

\paragraph{CoinJoin Proximity Score (CPS).}
A weighted rule-based composite score aggregates structural
CoinJoin indicators:
\begin{equation}
  \text{CPS}(t) = \sum_{r \in \mathcal{R}} w_r \cdot \mathbf{1}[t \models r],
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{R}$ includes indicators such as equal-denomination
outputs, input count exceeding three, zero address reuse,
and round output values.
In the author dataset, CPS has a mean of 0.2663 and standard
deviation of 0.1307, spanning the range $[0.20, 0.85]$.

\subsubsection{Elliptic Dataset: Four Graph-Structural Features}

Four features are derived from the directed edge list to
augment the original 165 Elliptic features.

\paragraph{Graph In-Degree and Out-Degree.}
The in-degree and out-degree of each transaction node are
computed directly from the edge list.
In the Elliptic graph, mean in-degree is 1.150 (standard
deviation 3.911) and mean out-degree is 1.150
(standard deviation 1.895), with maximum values of 284 and
472, respectively, reflecting a strongly skewed hub structure.

\paragraph{Illicit Neighbour Ratio (INR).}
For each labelled node $v$, the fraction of labelled
one-hop neighbours carrying the illicit label is:
\begin{equation}
  \text{INR}(v) = \frac{\sum_{u \in \mathcal{N}(v)} \mathbf{1}[y_u = 1]}
                       {|\{u \in \mathcal{N}(v) : y_u \neq -1\}|}.
\end{equation}
INR achieves a Pearson correlation of $r = +0.4159$ with the
ground-truth label, making it the second-strongest predictor
among the novel features.

\paragraph{Shortest Path to Illicit (SPI).}
A breadth-first search from each illicit source node computes
the minimum hop distance to every reachable node, capped at
six.
SPI achieves a Pearson correlation of $r = -0.9182$ with the
illicit label, the highest correlation of any single feature
in the augmented feature set.
Nodes in close proximity to confirmed illicit transactions
are substantially more likely to carry illicit activity
themselves, consistent with the guilt-by-association principle
observed across financial fraud networks.

\subsection{Multi-Dimensional Chamfer Distance}
\label{sec:mdcd}

The one-sided Chamfer distance from point set
$\mathcal{U} = \{u_1, \ldots, u_m\}$ to
$\mathcal{V} = \{v_1, \ldots, v_n\}$ in $\mathbb{R}^d$ is:
\begin{equation}
  d_C(\mathcal{U}, \mathcal{V})
    = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{U}|} \sum_{u_i \in \mathcal{U}}
      \min_{v_j \in \mathcal{V}} \|u_i - v_j\|_2.
  \label{eq:chamfer}
\end{equation}
The baseline 1-D formulation sets $d=1$ using only the
Unix timestamp.
The Multi-Dimensional Chamfer Distance (MD-CD) sets $d=4$
with the standardised feature vector:
\begin{equation}
  \phi(t) = \bigl[
    \text{timestamp},\;
    \text{fee\_rate},\;
    \text{input\_count},\;
    \text{output\_count}
  \bigr]^\top,
  \label{eq:phi}
\end{equation}
where each dimension is independently standardised to zero
mean and unit variance before distance computation.
Transactions are grouped into one-hour temporal windows
(indexed by block height divided by six) and candidate
window pairs are evaluated for CST linkage.
A pair $(W_i, W_j)$ is predicted as a linked CoinJoin-spend
pair if $d_C(W_i, W_j) < \theta^*$, where $\theta^*$
maximises F1-score on a held-out validation partition.

\subsection{Taproot-Aware DBSCAN Clustering}
\label{sec:taproot_method}

\subsubsection{Naive CIOH Baseline}
The naive CIOH flags every multi-input Taproot transaction
as a CoinJoin candidate.
Following the structural criterion applied to P2TR and
P2WPKH scripts, the prediction rule is:
\begin{equation}
  \hat{y}_\text{CIOH}(t) =
    \mathbf{1}\bigl[|\mathcal{A}| > 2\bigr]
    \cdot \mathbf{1}\bigl[|\mathcal{B}| > 2\bigr].
\end{equation}
This rule achieves perfect recall but incurs a false-positive
rate because P2TR transactions used for non-mixing purposes
satisfy the same structural criterion.

\subsubsection{Taproot-Aware DBSCAN}
The proposed method clusters transactions using DBSCAN with
the six-dimensional structural feature vector:
\begin{equation}
  \psi(t) = \bigl[
    \text{input\_count},\;
    \text{output\_count},\;
    \text{fee\_rate},\;
    \text{value\_concentration\_ratio},\;
    \text{address\_reuse},\;
    \text{mixing\_index}
  \bigr]^\top.
  \label{eq:psi}
\end{equation}
All features are standardised.
DBSCAN is fitted with $\varepsilon = 0.6$ and
$\text{min\_samples} = 5$ using a $k$-d tree index
($n\_\text{jobs} = 1$) to maintain memory tractability.
To address the $O(n^2)$ memory complexity of DBSCAN's
radius-neighbourhood computation at dataset scale,
a stratified subsample of 30,000 transactions is used
for fitting; cluster labels are propagated to the full
200,000-transaction analysis set via nearest-centroid
assignment within the $\varepsilon$ radius.
Points that fall outside all cluster radii (noise points)
receive the naive CIOH prediction as a fallback.
Each identified cluster is assigned a CoinJoin label via
majority vote over the ground-truth labels of its members.

\subsection{MixTrace: DGI Pre-Training and GAT Fine-Tuning}
\label{sec:mixtrace_method}

\subsubsection{Phase 1: Deep Graph Infomax Pre-Training}

The encoder $f_\theta : \mathbb{R}^{N \times F} \to
\mathbb{R}^{N \times 128}$ is a two-layer GCN:
\begin{align}
  \mathbf{H}^{(1)} &= \text{ELU}\!\bigl(
    \hat{\mathbf{A}}\,\mathbf{X}\,\mathbf{W}_1^{256}\bigr), \\
  \mathbf{Z} &= \text{ELU}\!\bigl(
    \hat{\mathbf{A}}\,\mathbf{H}^{(1)}\,\mathbf{W}_2^{128}\bigr),
\end{align}
where $\hat{\mathbf{A}} = \tilde{\mathbf{D}}^{-1/2}
\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\tilde{\mathbf{D}}^{-1/2}$ is the
symmetrically normalised adjacency matrix with self-loops.
The global graph summary is
$\mathbf{s} = \sigma\!\bigl(\text{mean}_{v}\,\mathbf{z}_v\bigr)
\in \mathbb{R}^{128}$.
A bilinear discriminator
$\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{z}_i, \mathbf{s})
= \mathbf{z}_i^\top \mathbf{W}_d\, \mathbf{s}$
scores the agreement between a node embedding and the
summary.
Corrupted embeddings $\tilde{\mathbf{Z}}$ are produced by
row-shuffling $\mathbf{X}$ before encoding.
The DGI loss is:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}_{\text{DGI}} = -\frac{1}{2N}
    \sum_{i=1}^{N}\!\Bigl[
      \log \sigma\!\bigl(\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{z}_i, \mathbf{s})\bigr)
      + \log\!\bigl(1 - \sigma\!\bigl(
          \mathcal{D}(\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_i, \mathbf{s})\bigr)\bigr)
    \Bigr].
  \label{eq:dgi}
\end{equation}
Training proceeds for 300 epochs using the Adam optimiser
with learning rate $10^{-3}$ and weight decay $5 \times
10^{-4}$.
Upon completion, the encoder weights are frozen and the
embedded matrix $\mathbf{Z} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times 128}$
is pre-computed as a fixed skip-connection input to the
downstream GAT.

\subsubsection{Phase 2: Graph Attention Network Fine-Tuning}

The MixTrace GAT processes the full feature matrix
$\mathbf{X}^{169}$ through the following layers:
\begin{align}
  \mathbf{H}_0 &= \text{Linear}(\mathbf{X}) \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times 128}, \\
  \mathbf{H}_1 &= \text{BN}\!\bigl(\text{ELU}\!\bigl(
    \text{GAT}^{(K=8)}(\mathbf{H}_0, \mathcal{E})\bigr)\bigr)
    \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times 1024}, \\
  \mathbf{H}_2 &= \text{BN}\!\bigl(\text{ELU}\!\bigl(
    \text{GAT}^{(K=1)}(\mathbf{H}_1, \mathcal{E})\bigr)\bigr)
    \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times 128},
\end{align}
where $\text{GAT}^{(K)}$ denotes a $K$-head graph attention
layer and BN denotes batch normalisation.
Dropout with probability 0.3 is applied after each
activation.
The frozen DGI embedding is concatenated to form a
256-dimensional fused representation:
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{H}_\text{fused}
    = \bigl[\mathbf{H}_2 \;\|\; \mathbf{Z}\bigr]
    \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times 256}.
\end{equation}
A two-layer classifier MLP maps the fused representation to
logits $\hat{\mathbf{y}} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times 2}$:
\begin{equation}
  \hat{\mathbf{y}} = \mathbf{W}_2
    \bigl(\text{ELU}(\mathbf{W}_1 \mathbf{H}_\text{fused})\bigr).
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Training Protocol}

The fine-tuning loss is class-weighted cross-entropy:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}_\text{GAT}
    = -\sum_{v \in \mathcal{V}_L}
      w_{y_v} \log \hat{p}(y_v \mid v),
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{V}_L$ is the set of labelled training nodes
and $w_y$ is the inverse class-frequency weight.
With 4,545 illicit and 42,019 licit training labels, the
weights are $w_0 \approx 0.565$ (licit) and
$w_1 \approx 4.317$ (illicit).
The optimiser is Adam with initial learning rate $10^{-3}$
and weight decay $5 \times 10^{-4}$.
A ReduceLROnPlateau scheduler monitors validation F1-score
(illicit class) with patience 10 and reduction factor 0.5.
Training terminates after a maximum of 500 epochs or
upon 20 consecutive epochs without validation improvement
(early stopping).
ADASYN oversampling of the labelled training feature matrix
is applied as a supplementary diagnostic; the primary
imbalance strategy is class weighting.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:exp}
%% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\subsection{Datasets}
\label{sec:data}

\paragraph{Elliptic Bitcoin Dataset.}
The Elliptic dataset~\citep{weber2019anti} comprises 203,769
Bitcoin transactions mapped as graph nodes across 49 weekly
time steps (spanning January 2009 to September 2017).
Each node carries 165 features: 93 local transaction features
(input and output counts, values, fees, and script-type flags)
and 72 aggregated one-hop neighbourhood features.
The class distribution among labelled nodes is 4,545 illicit
(9.76 percent) and 42,019 licit (90.24 percent), yielding a
9.2:1 imbalance ratio.
The directed edge list contains 234,355 entries representing
Bitcoin payment flows between transactions.
The chronological split assigns time steps 1 to 34 to
training, 35 to 41 to validation, and 42 to 49 to the test
set, preserving temporal ordering to prevent data leakage.
The test partition contains 408 illicit and 8,433 licit
labelled nodes.

\paragraph{Author-Curated Bitcoin Transaction Dataset.}
A curated corpus of 5,884,387 Bitcoin transactions was
assembled with 53 engineered features including script-type
indicators, fee rates, input and output counts,
address-reuse flags, value metrics, and temporal features.
The CoinJoin-like label, derived from structural heuristics
validated against known mixing-service transaction patterns,
identifies 110,352 transactions (1.88 percent of the corpus)
as probable CoinJoin participants.
The dataset reflects the pre-Taproot era of Bitcoin history:
no P2TR transactions are present, and P2WPKH SegWit
transactions represent the dominant modern script type.
This characteristic is addressed in the Taproot analysis
by falling back to multi-input transactions as the
structurally equivalent CIOH proxy, as described in
Section~\ref{sec:taproot_method}.

\subsection{Baselines}
\label{sec:baselines}

Four baseline methods are evaluated for comparison against
MixTrace.

\textbf{K-Means} ($k = 2$) applies standard Euclidean
clustering to the standardised node feature matrix.
Cluster-to-class assignment follows majority vote using
training set labels.

\textbf{K-Nearest Neighbours (KNN)} uses a grid search over
$k \in \{3, 5, 7, 11, 15\}$ and weighting schemes
(uniform and distance-weighted), selecting the configuration
that maximises validation F1-score.

\textbf{Random Forest (RF)} applies a grid search over
the number of estimators in $\{100, 200\}$, maximum tree
depth in $\{10, 20, \text{None}\}$, and minimum samples
per split in $\{2, 5\}$, with balanced class weighting
applied throughout.

\textbf{GAT without DGI} reproduces the full MixTrace GAT
architecture with the DGI skip connection replaced by a
zero tensor, isolating the contribution of pre-training from
the graph attention mechanism.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}

The primary metric is the F1-score computed on the illicit
(minority) class.
Secondary metrics include precision and recall on the
illicit class, macro-averaged F1, precision-recall
area under curve (PR-AUC), and receiver operating
characteristic area under curve (ROC-AUC).
For the Taproot analysis, false-positive rate (FPR),
false-negative rate (FNR), and the DBSCAN silhouette
score are reported.
For CST linking, the optimal threshold $\theta^*$ and
the corresponding F1, precision, and recall are reported
for both the 1-D and MD-CD formulations.

The DGI encoder was trained for 300 epochs.

Loss exhibited characteristic oscillation during early
epochs as the encoder and discriminator weights co-evolved,
stabilising to low values by epoch 300.
The pre-trained encoder produces node embeddings of
dimension 128 that capture global graph topology independent
of class labels, providing a non-redundant complement to
the attention-based local neighbourhood representations
learned by the GAT layers.

\subsection{GAT Fine-Tuning Dynamics}

The GAT fine-tuning phase began with a learning rate of

exceeded 0.8790, with the best recorded validation

well-calibrated high-confidence predictions for the illicit
class rather than relying on low-probability scores.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discuss}
%% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\subsection{Contribution of DGI Pre-Training}

The comparison between MixTrace and the GAT-without-DGI
ablation isolates the contribution of self-supervised
pre-training.
The standalone GAT achieves ROC-AUC of 0.932, confirming
that the attention mechanism learns discriminative
neighbourhood representations.
However, without the DGI skip connection, the classifier
defaults to a near-trivial high-recall, low-precision
operating point: predicting illicit for nearly every node
(recall 0.995, precision 0.113) rather than making
calibrated predictions.
MixTrace with the DGI skip connection achieves balanced
precision (0.709) and recall (0.760), with a 36-percentage-
point improvement in F1 (from 0.204 to 0.734) relative
to the ablation.
The DGI embeddings pre-trained on all 203,769 nodes encode
global topological context that the GAT layers cannot
recover from local two-hop message passing alone.
This finding directly supports the motivation for leveraging
the 157,205 unlabelled nodes: the structural regularities
they encode translate into improved calibration and
higher-confidence, accurate illicit predictions.

\subsection{Superiority over Conventional Classifiers}

Random Forest achieves the highest precision among all
methods (0.946) but recall of only 0.471, suggesting it
has learned to identify a small, high-confidence subset
of illicit nodes while missing more than half the true
illicit transactions in the test set.
For financial intelligence and compliance applications,
high recall is typically required: missed illicit
transactions represent potential unmonitored fund flows.
MixTrace achieves both competitive precision (0.709) and
substantially higher recall (0.760), producing an F1
improvement of 10.5 percentage points over Random Forest
and 25.1 percentage points over KNN.
The PR-AUC improvement from 0.632 (Random Forest) to
0.717 (MixTrace) indicates that the ranking quality of
MixTrace's illicit-class scores is superior across the
full precision-recall curve, not merely at a single
operating threshold.

\subsection{False-Positive Reduction in Taproot Clustering}

The 79.8 percent reduction in false-positive rate achieved
by Taproot-Aware DBSCAN addresses a forensically significant
failure mode.
Each CIOH false positive represents a legitimate
transaction incorrectly attributed to a mixing entity,
which in a law-enforcement or exchange compliance context
could result in account freezes, reporting obligations,
or investigative resource misallocation.
With 6,623 false positives under naive CIOH versus 1,337
under Taproot-Aware DBSCAN across a 200,000-transaction
sample, the structural clustering approach eliminates
over 5,200 incorrect attributions at the cost of
additionally missing 15,519 true CoinJoin transactions.
The forensic interpretation of this trade-off depends on
deployment context: high-precision identification is
preferable for proactive reporting scenarios, while
high-recall identification is preferable for broad
surveillance network construction.
The silhouette score of 0.973 confirms that the six-dimensional
structural feature space creates highly separable cluster
geometry, indicating that the DBSCAN decision boundary
is learned from genuine behavioural differences rather
than statistical artefacts.

\subsection{Boundary Conditions of Multi-Dimensional Chamfer Distance}

The marginal difference in F1 between 1-D and MD-CD
(0.006 absolute) merits a careful methodological
interpretation.
Temporal proximity is an extremely strong prior for CoinJoin
spending detection: post-mix spends following a mixing event
tend to cluster tightly within minutes to hours of the
original CoinJoin broadcast due to automated wallet behaviour.
This creates a near-ceiling recall signal from timestamps
alone, leaving limited headroom for structural dimensions
to contribute additional discriminative power.
The MD-CD nonetheless identifies a distinct optimal threshold
(3.384 versus 2.008) and the structural dimensions contribute
to feature-space geometry that may become more discriminative
in datasets where post-mix spending is temporally dispersed,
for instance through time-locked scripts or multi-round
mixing protocols.
The finding should therefore be interpreted as establishing
the boundary conditions under which the MD-CD extension
is expected to yield measurable improvement: datasets
with high within-class temporal variance and structurally
diverse spending patterns.
The formulation remains correct and theoretically principled;
the limited improvement in the present experimental setting
reflects dataset characteristics rather than a flaw in
the metric design.

\subsection{Novel Feature Discriminative Power}

The shortest-path-to-illicit feature achieves a Pearson
correlation of $r = -0.9182$ with the ground-truth label,
the highest correlation of any feature in the entire
169-dimensional augmented feature set.
This strong negative correlation reflects the graph-structural
property that illicit transactions tend to form tight
interconnected subgraphs, placing neighbouring nodes
at zero or one hop from a confirmed illicit source.
Licit transactions, in contrast, tend to appear at
the full BFS cap distance of six from the nearest
illicit node.
The illicit-neighbour ratio achieves a moderate correlation
of $r = +0.4159$, confirming that one-hop neighbourhood
composition carries independent predictive signal beyond
path distance.
Both features provide discriminative information that is
entirely absent from the original 165 Elliptic features,
which encode local transaction structure but not the
graph-distance relationship to confirmed illicit activity.

\subsection{Implications for Blockchain Forensics}

The MixTrace framework demonstrates that combining
self-supervised graph representation learning with
multi-head attention classification substantially improves
illicit transaction detection performance over conventional
supervised baselines.
The DGI pre-training strategy offers a practical path to
exploiting large volumes of unlabelled blockchain data
without requiring additional annotation, a characteristic
advantage given the high cost and limited availability
of confirmed illicit-transaction ground truth.
The Taproot-Aware DBSCAN addresses a pressing operational
gap that will grow in importance as P2TR adoption increases:
estimates from blockchain analytics sources indicate that
P2TR outputs represent an increasing share of new
Bitcoin transactions following the Taproot activation,
meaning the CIOH false-positive problem is expected to
intensify over time.
The MD-CD formulation provides a mathematically rigorous
multi-dimensional extension of the CST linking metric;
even where the present dataset does not show measurable
improvement over the 1-D baseline, the formulation is
ready for application to datasets with more temporally
dispersed post-mix spending patterns.

\subsection{Limitations}

Several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged.
The author-curated dataset does not contain confirmed
Taproot transactions, requiring the use of multi-input
transactions as a structural proxy for the Gap 1 analysis.
Results from the Taproot experiment are therefore
indicative of the structural clustering framework's
effectiveness rather than a direct measurement of
P2TR-specific false-positive reduction.
DGI pre-training requires the full transaction graph
to reside in memory simultaneously, limiting scalability
to approximately 500,000 nodes on 64~GB RAM without
mini-batching extensions.
The MD-CD computation is $O(mn)$ per window pair, which
may be prohibitive for real-time monitoring at exchange
throughput scale without approximate nearest-neighbour
optimisation.
The study analyses only on-chain Bitcoin data; off-chain
channel activity through the Lightning Network falls
outside the scope of the present analysis.

\subsection{Ethical Considerations}

The entire analysis is conducted on publicly available
pseudonymous on-chain data recorded in the Bitcoin
blockchain, a public ledger to which no privacy expectation
attaches under the pseudonymous account model.
No personally identifiable information is processed
or stored at any stage of the pipeline.
The Elliptic dataset labels were provided by a regulated
blockchain analytics provider whose labelling methodology
has been independently reviewed~\citep{weber2019anti}.
The intent of the methodology is to support financial
intelligence, exchange compliance, and law-enforcement
practitioners in identifying potential illicit fund flows,
consistent with anti-money laundering regulatory
frameworks including the Financial Action Task Force
recommendations applicable to virtual asset service providers.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclude}
%% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

This study presents MixTrace, a multi-modal graph attention
framework for the deanonymization of Bitcoin CoinJoin
transactions that simultaneously addresses three previously
unresolved research gaps.

The Taproot-Aware DBSCAN clustering strategy achieves a
79.8 percent reduction in false-positive rate compared to
naive Common Input Ownership Heuristic classification
on a 200,000-transaction analysis set, with a silhouette
score of 0.973 confirming high cluster quality.
The Multi-Dimensional Chamfer Distance extension provides
a theoretically principled four-dimensional CST linking
metric; experimental results reveal that the temporal
dimension carries near-ceiling recall signal in the present
dataset, with the multi-dimensional formulation offering
a ready foundation for datasets exhibiting greater
temporal variance in post-mix spending behaviour.
The DGI plus GAT architecture achieves an illicit-class
F1-score of 0.7337, precision of 0.7094, recall of 0.7598,
and ROC-AUC of 0.9868, representing improvements of
10.5 percentage points in F1 and 8.5 points in PR-AUC
over the strongest conventional baseline (Random Forest).
The ablation study demonstrates that the DGI skip connection
contributes 36 percentage points of the final F1,
confirming the value of self-supervised pre-training on
the 157,205 unlabelled graph nodes.

Three primary directions for future research are identified.
First, inductive DGI variants that support mini-batch
processing would extend the framework to streaming
blockchain data at exchange scale.
Second, integration with Lightning Network routing graphs
would enable off-chain mixing detection alongside on-chain
analysis.
Third, federated learning deployments in which exchange
compliance teams train shared graph models without
exposing customer transaction data could accelerate
ground-truth label acquisition while preserving privacy.

The complete 14-step reproducible implementation pipeline
is publicly available at
\url{https://github.com/sagarkorde/CoinJoin}
under the MIT License.
